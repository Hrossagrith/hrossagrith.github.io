Transport and roads

A country whose road culture is built on equids, and the one narrow exception it makes for motor freight.

Hrossagrið fundamentally does not want ordinary motor roads. Its public roads are built around ridden and driven equids, and every animal entering one carries a road-status marker declaring what other road users must do about it.

Hrossaway

The exception, and why it exists

The Empire also exists in the real world. It needs imports, exports, medicine, industrial equipment, raw materials and international trade. It could refuse all motor freight on principle — and then damage its economy, complicate the supply of medical equipment, and create a great deal of new harm. That would be a strange outcome for a system whose whole purpose is to minimize destruction.

Hrossaway is the resulting narrow exception: a closed motor-freight road running from an international port or border freight point to a specific transfer terminal. A truck enters. It unloads. From there goods continue by rail or by heavy horse-drawn freight teams. The principle is not abandoned, and it is also not turned into a suicidal dogma.

Rail

Long-distance, high-speed and electric — and nothing else

Rail is already written into the register. The Imperial Road and Driver Code limits the Empire’s mechanized public land transport to long-distance high-speed electric rail on dedicated mainline tracks. There is no metro, no underground urban railway, no tramway, no suburban commuter railway, no light rail, no local rapid-transit railway, and no diesel or steam service. Rail carries travel between distant regions and principal settlements; movement inside a settlement, and between a station and the district around it, happens on foot, by ridden or led equid, or by equid-drawn vehicle.

The same Code separates the two networks physically. High-speed railway corridors are kept apart from equid roads, settlements, pastures and public paths, and a public road crosses an active high-speed line only by grade-separated bridge or underpass. No level crossing, road-running rail, shared tramway surface or mixed rail-and-equid corridor is permitted. Stations, access roads, loading areas and service yards are designed so that train noise, air movement, vibration, lighting, barriers, crowds and sudden movement do not create avoidable danger to an equid.

Carriages and emergency transport

Where the binding detail lives

Carriage work is governed in full by the Imperial Code for the Welfare of Working Carriage Equids: passports, certification, workload and rest, weather burden, route safety, harness, headgear mounts, road-status inserts, lighting and ground-guidance lamps. Ambulance carriages have their own technical standard covering speed, low jostle, suspension, emergency lanes and glideways.

Manure

An organic-resource stream, not a street-cleaning problem

A country whose transport runs on equids produces manure at municipal-waste scale. The archive therefore refuses to treat it as an incidental matter for street cleaners and plans it as a national organic-resource stream, on the model used for sewage, compost feedstock and agricultural nutrient management.

The order of operations is source capture first, municipal cleanup second. Regularly working carriage horses use carriage-mounted manure-catching systems, so that waste is taken before it reaches the road; ridden and led horses are subject to immediate handler pickup where practical, with portable sealed collection equipment; and dense urban areas, carriage ranks, freight yards, stables, public equid facilities and Rest Courts hold standard sealed manure-transfer containers. Municipal equid-street sanitation remains necessary — for misses, urine, accidents, loose animals, events and general washing — but it is not expected to collect every defecation by hand. The planning target is very high source capture, on the order of 95 percent in dense managed systems, and the model is explicit that the figure is to be tested rather than assumed. If it holds, even a very horse-dense capital needs hundreds of dedicated equid-street sanitation workers rather than tens of thousands, because route coverage and cleaning frequency matter more than the raw weight of manure left on the streets.

Material from municipal depots, stables, carriage terminals, freight yards, riding establishments, government horses and agricultural users goes to regional Equid Organic Resource Centers, which separate clean manure from street grit and contamination and route it by quality and local demand:

  • compost and agricultural soil amendment
  • pelletized or dried fertilizer products
  • mushroom-substrate production where suitable
  • anaerobic digestion for biogas and digestate where technically appropriate
  • pyrolysis to biochar, for surplus material and for carbon-rich industrial use

Processed fertilizer, compost, biochar and manure-processing technology are expected to become major exports; wet raw manure is not, being bulky, low-value freight. Domestic agricultural use is nutrient-budgeted rather than unlimited. Horse manure carries useful nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, but over-application pollutes soil and water, so the state maps regional nutrient demand and prevents the national horse population from becoming a mechanism for fertilizing rivers; surplus is processed, stored, moved to deficient regions or exported.

Urine needs its own infrastructure, because it spreads, penetrates surfaces and creates ammonia and drainage problems. Major horse facilities are to have hoof-safe relief areas, absorbent or draining surfaces where appropriate, and sealed collection or controlled wastewater connections, and street drainage is designed on the assumption that equids will sometimes urinate on the road. Horse-contaminated wash water is not simply flushed into natural waterways.

Abundance is not an argument for paving with it. Raw manure is not to be added to ordinary road surfaces, and any manure-derived material proposed for road sub-base, drainage, stormwater filtration, soil stabilization or hoof-road construction must first be converted into a standardized industrial material such as biochar, then pass load, water, freeze-thaw, dust, durability and hoof-health testing. The stated rule is that if it sounds useful it gets tested, not canonized by enthusiasm.

All of the above is held in the archive as a working national model rather than enacted law. It is written as what should be done; no manure code, resource-center standard or capture requirement is yet in force.

In preparation. Road authority and infrastructure law — construction, maintenance and control of the public road network — is reserved for separate drafting so that carriage welfare law does not absorb it. Reserved with it, intended but neither built nor written: horse roads and carriage lanes, approved routes, Rest Courts, waiting bays, water points, forage points where needed, shade and shelter, pull-over areas, emergency release spaces, emergency lanes, glideways, sleigh-compatible winter routes, runner and wheel channels, separate hoof-safe traction strips, surfaces that carry vehicle speed without breaking hooves, rules for bridges, hills, descents, cobbles, metal surfaces, ice, mud, rail grooves and tramlines, market, crowd and event routing, and ambulance relay stations with rest teams.

On the roads Every equid on a public road wears approved bitless headgear with a Facial Marker.
