Book IV

Early history

Four reconstructed parts covering c. 1200–220 BC. The account does not begin with a desired modern territory, and it does not grant the Empire a national birth the evidence cannot carry.

Historical method. This reconstruction combines real archaeological constraints with a fictional Hrossagrith tradition. Archaeology can constrain environments, technologies, settlement patterns and material exchange; it cannot verify the particular feuds, decisions, speeches or motives recorded here. Where the surviving tradition cannot distinguish among several explanations, the account preserves that uncertainty rather than presenting one motive as established fact.

c. 1200–980 BC Part I

Before the people, and the crisis that made them

  1. c. 1200–1050 BC

    Before the Hrossagrith people

    There is no Hrossagrith people, state or common identity. The central Irish lowlands hold scattered farming communities around the middle Shannon, Lough Ree, central Westmeath, eastern Roscommon and northern Offaly. Authority belongs to households, kin groups and local leaders rather than permanent states. Horses may be present in small numbers, but their exact role is uncertain; they are not yet the basis of society, law or government — and the region that later becomes the Hrossagrith core is one ordinary inland area among several wealthier ones.

    Territorial status: no Compact, no shared territory.

  2. c. 1050–980 BC

    The Crossing Crisis

    The first lasting divergence is placed at an important crossing on the middle Shannon — already more than a practical route, used for seasonal exchange, marriage arrangements, ritual gatherings, dispute settlement and the movement of livestock and travelers.

    Later traditions combine a succession of disputes over stolen cattle, access to the crossing and old killings into a wider feud; whether these were one connected conflict from the beginning is uncertain. Retaliation spreads beyond the original families; travelers are attacked, repairs stop, and the crossing becomes unusable. Everyone nearby suffers: livestock cannot be moved safely, traders avoid the route, isolated settlements become vulnerable.

    Across several later gatherings, influential households repeatedly restore and extend a temporary peace custom — approximately, a prohibition on killing or seizing an accepted participant during a gathering or on its recognized approaches. The protection is narrow: only during the gathering, only in recognized places, only for people the hosts accept.

c. 650–500 BC Part II

A small and unreliable network, repeatedly recreated

  1. c. 650–620 BC

    Survival and unreliable memory

    One group repairs a crossing, another keeps boats ready, another clears higher paths over gravel ridges, another stores fodder. The result is an uneven network of usable routes, not a planned road system. There is no written archive: agreements are repeated publicly before witnesses and marked by exchanged objects, pledged livestock or recognized landmarks. Witnesses die, objects are lost, powerful households intimidate people, and settlements preserve incompatible versions of the same promise.

    Sanctuary also fails. Protected travelers are occasionally robbed or killed, sometimes by people connected to their own hosts. What makes sanctuary valuable is not that it is never broken, but that breaking it can bring retaliation from several unrelated communities and damage future trade.

  2. c. 620–590 BC

    Craft households and disputed dependence

    Iron arrives unevenly, through several people and routes rather than one technological change; bronze stays valuable and there is no dependable Compact iron industry. Traveling craft households are welcomed selectively for their skills, and sponsors provide food, protection and a place to work in exchange for labor, goods or teaching — arrangements that repeatedly cause disputes when the amount, duration or consent was never clearly established.

    Over decades a custom strengthens: a person accepted under protection should not be separated from accompanying household members, or deprived of protected animals and essential objects, before the dispute is brought before witnesses. Powerful sponsors sometimes ignore it entirely.

  3. c. 590–550 BC

    Escort payment disputes and combined backing

    Households providing escorts, boats and lookouts demand more compensation. They are not professional soldiers but farming and herding households diverting labor and food while risking lives. When an influential escort lineage is accused of taking additional payments from travelers already promised protection, hosts object that this turns a guarantee into extortion. Neither side secures a lasting victory.

    What emerges instead is a durable practical lesson: agreements backed by both kinds of social power endure — people able to assemble witnesses and negotiate obligations, and people able to organize escorts, retaliation and defense. This is a habit, not a diarchy, and it produces no office.

  4. c. 550–500 BC

    A durable but disputed network

    Households from different origins are connected by marriages, fostered children, debts, feuds and craft ties, but no clear common identity survives in the record. Some expectations outlast the emergencies that created them: aid is repaid, jointly maintained crossings are not obstructed without warning, and attacking a protected guest may draw retaliation from communities unrelated to the victim.

    Influential customs continue to reject permanent human ownership and the transfer of people as property. Detention and temporary hostages occur, but those held remain protected persons who must be released when the obligation is fulfilled or formally abandoned.

    Horses remain poorly evidenced and socially limited, and are not yet central to any identity. Safe-conduct customs protect animals traveling with a household, including any horses present, because their seizure can trap or impoverish protected people.

    Territorial status: a fragile protection network around the middle Shannon — without the name Hrossagrið, a monarch, either Crown, written law, a permanent army, a capital, a continuous border or an Atlantic harbor or landing place.

A promise of protection means little unless someone can make it physically real, while force without a recognized promise becomes extortion.

c. 500–300 BC Part III

Renewal, over-reach, failure, and the first political horses

  1. c. 500–460 BC

    Renewal without centralization

    Sanctuary practices continue because they solve recurring problems: strangers need sponsors, traders need safe stopping places, hosts need compensation, and neighbors need ways to end feuds without surrendering entirely. One seasonal gathering becomes more regular — not because anyone founds a capital, but because its location has water, grazing and familiar approaches, and host households compete for the prestige it brings. In years of shortage it shrinks, moves, or is abandoned.

  2. c. 460–420 BC

    The Three-Routes Agreement

    The original crossing becomes a bottleneck in good seasons and a point of failure in bad ones. Influential households try to connect three alternatives: the old crossing, an upstream boat passage, and an east–west path over firmer raised ground, coordinating escort contributions, boat repair, compensation and emergency grazing.

    It works for a time, because no single route is indispensable. Its success then creates new conflicts: contributions are unequal, households near the busiest crossing collect more gifts and prestige, and contributors answer free-riding by reserving boats and escorts for their own people. The agreement does not collapse in a moment — contributions become irregular, gatherings are attended selectively, and within a generation common coordination has mostly failed.

    Territorial status: three routes briefly connected by overlapping promises. They never become one governed district.

  3. c. 420–370 BC

    Fodder years and the limits of refuge

    Several damaging local seasons within a generation — flooded grazing, fodder shortage, livestock disease, interrupted exchange — are adopted as a plausible compound shock rather than a dated climate event. The refuge network cannot accept everyone. Hosts ration protection: skilled workers and people with marriage connections are more likely to be admitted than unsupported families.

    The pressure exposes the limits of the anti-ownership custom. Sponsors try to convert unpaid food debts into open-ended service, or to keep younger household members after an agreement ends. The conflict strengthens an uneven rule that continued service requires renewal before witnesses, with the person expected to serve present and their assent heard — though pressure can still make assent a fiction.

  4. c. 330–300 BC

    Politically significant horse keeping

    Direct evidence is too sparse to establish how many horses were kept, who controlled them, or whether mounted messaging existed in the middle Shannon around c. 300 BC. The adopted reconstruction therefore has a few wealthy protection households gradually divert grazing, winter fodder and labor to horses, because repeated route disputes make speed valuable. Breeding does not quickly enlarge the stock: a mare carries a foal through most of a year, and a surviving young horse consumes several seasons before it can carry an urgent rider. A concentration can vanish within a generation through injury, disease or the loss of a mare.

    The advantage is nevertheless political. A mounted messenger can warn a crossing, summon relatives, or reach a gathering before a rival account of a dispute. But speed does not make a message true: later witnesses may contradict it, and hosts may treat an unsupported mounted report as manipulation.

    Territorial status by c. 300 BC: more recognized customs, a few wealthy lineages, uneven ironworking — and still no common national name, monarch, Crown, permanent assembly, written law, common army, common taxation, coinage, city, fixed border, Atlantic harbor or large horse force.

c. 300–220 BC Part IV

Speed, and what it could not buy

  1. c. 300–260 BC

    The cost of acting first

    Mounted messengers create an immediate political problem, and the answer is not a mounted ruling class. Suitable horses are scarce, and owning one is not the same as keeping it usable: fodder must be stored, injuries managed, riders must learn the ground. Wet ground, flooding, darkness and water crossings erase much of the speed advantage.

    The deeper change is that important hosts increasingly refuse to treat first arrival as proof. A messenger may secure temporary precautions, but a lasting judgment waits for named witnesses or a second account — which reduces the benefit of racing to establish a false story first.

    In disputes where communication or protected travel matters, horse seizure becomes politically charged in a way the loss of an ordinary animal may not be. Cattle remain far more important to most households as wealth, food and breeding stock, but taking a rival’s horse can also remove speed, prestige and the ability to summon allies. There is still no general doctrine that horses are legally unlike cattle, compensation depends on circumstance, and communities outside the network may see no reason to recognize any special distinction at all.

    Territorial status: protected places and routes embedded among independently controlled lands. A person may move from one recognized host to another without ever entering a continuous jurisdiction.

  2. c. 260–220 BC

    The horse-guarantee disputes

    As horses become more valuable for communication, disagreement arises over whether a sanctuary promise protecting a traveler also protects that traveler’s mount from seizure, ransom, retaliation or forced use. Earlier refuge practice had often treated animals and goods as part of the arrangement, but never through one consistent rule. A cluster of influential hosts begins insisting that animals explicitly declared with a traveler are covered by the promise, and that participants who recognize the guarantee are expected to respect the same restraint while it lasts. This is not animal rights in a modern sense; it follows from the credibility of safe conduct, because a person is not truly safe if their means of departure can be confiscated immediately.

    The custom stays narrow and contested. It applies most reliably to declared mounts, pack animals and animals needed for the protected journey. It does not imply that every horse everywhere is protected from sale, slaughter, harsh treatment or requisition, and it does not prevent a host from demanding food, labor or compensation for keeping an animal through a long stay. Abuse follows quickly: travelers declare stolen animals as their own protected mounts, hosts claim an animal was never included in the guarantee, and powerful sponsors treat an unpaid maintenance debt as title to the horse — which opponents answer destroys the entire point of safe conduct.

    The most durable response is procedural rather than moral. Before witnesses, a host receiving an important traveler increasingly names the person, the declared animals, the expected duration and any agreed contribution for fodder or care, and may repeat a visible description — color, sex, markings, age, scars, unusual tack — so that a substitute animal cannot easily be inserted later. These descriptions are oral and imprecise, and similar-looking animals, seasonal coat changes, poor memory and deliberate lying still create disputes. Where an outside claimant pursues an allegedly stolen horse, some hosts hold the disputed animal apart from both parties while witnesses are sought; no permanent tribunal develops, and neither this practice nor the reputational cost of breaking a declared-animal guarantee creates a national horse ethic by itself.

    Territorial status: the practical scope of protection becomes slightly clearer at important host settlements, but it remains attached to people and declared journeys. No border marks where a general horse-protection law begins.

On the name, and on the Crowns. Throughout this period the name Hrossagrið does not exist; it emerges much later as an Old Norse outsider-name. The recurring distinction between people who negotiate obligations and people who organize protection is treated by historians only as a faint cultural ancestor of the Guiding and Guarding Crowns, which emerge much later from separate crises. No unbroken office connects them.

Names used here For the early period historians use the modern labels Sanctuary Compact and Compact communities. No contemporary collective name survives.

What is invented The feuds, decisions and institutions. Not the archaeology, and not the grammar of the later name.
