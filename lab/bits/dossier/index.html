  • Thickness check: in a space. The mouthpiece fits without crowding the tongue at rest.
  • Single joint: under tension the two halves of the mouthpiece fold into a V. Measured: under tension, correctly sized jointed snaffles move away from the palate and into the tongue (Engelke & Gasse 2002, cadavers; Manfredi et al. 2005, radiographs). From statics: the tongue is squeezed between the halves, and their ends bear on the bars at an angle. Palate contact needs a bit that is too wide or a joint lifted with little tension (palate index ).
  • Two joints: the centre piece lies on the tongue and the halves follow its sides. Model assumption: the mouthpiece settles onto the tongue rather than rising towards the palate. Pressure is spread, not removed.
  • Model assumption: a flat plate loads the tongue evenly only while it lies flat. A cheek that resists rotation, or a rotated shank, can tilt the plate so that its edge bears on the tongue; in the mouth a French link then acts more like a Dr Bristol.
  • Unjointed: the bar cannot fold. Once it has pressed the relaxed tongue down, most further load goes to the two bars, where it rests on thin mucosa over bone; a braced tongue carries more. Measured: bar lesions were more frequent with unjointed bits in Finnish trotters (Tuomola et al. 2021).
  • The port gives the tongue room and sends even more of the load to the bars. When the shank rotates (curb action) the port swings upward. Model assumption: a medium or high port reaches the palate at ° of rotation (palate index ).
  • By construction this mouthpiece touches the palate: the spoon or U-piece stands tall and meets the roof of the mouth as soon as the shank rotates or the head is pressed against it. Palate contact is part of how it works, not a side effect.
  • Textured surface: only of the surface touches tissue, and the ridges concentrate the load (edge factor ). A thinner mouthpiece also makes a narrower contact strip on the bars. Model assumption: edge factors and contact fractions are estimates, not measurements.
  • Many links: the mouthpiece drapes over the tongue and cannot be leaned on, but each link is a small contact. Not studied: whether the gaps between links trap tongue or lip tissue.
  • Two strands with offset joints: the tongue is caught between four half-cannons that fold independently, and the load is carried on two thin lines instead of one.
  • The two halves cross and slide past each other: a one-sided rein draws one half across the tongue against the other, so by construction the tongue can be pinched.
  • A plate under the joint spreads the centre load over the tongue and stops the joint pinching it; the extended version also holds the tongue down, which restrains the tongue rather than relieving it.
  • Keys hang from the bar. The bar itself is a straight bar; the keys add movement, not pressure relief. Not studied: whether keys change chewing or acceptance of the bit.
  • A soft or covered mouthpiece conforms to the tongue and spreads the load; it is also thick, wears, and is chewed through, so it must be inspected for an exposed core.
  • The mouthpiece turns freely on its ring: pulled from below it presents its edge to the tongue and bars (edge factor raised to ).

Figure D

Is there a stop, or does it keep going?

Two different questions. Does anything mechanical limit how far the bit moves in the mouth as the rider pulls harder, and does anything limit the force? A chain stops a shank rotating but not the force it transmits. A cheekpiece strap can only pull, so no cheek, loose, fixed, full, D or hanging, stops the mouthpiece being drawn into the corners of the mouth when the rein has a component towards the poll; under rein tension a hanging cheek moved towards the cheek teeth as much as a jointed loose-ring snaffle (Manfredi et al. 2005). A sliding gag has no stop at all before the corners of the mouth and the first cheek teeth; a ring around the jaw stops on bone.

  • Nothing mechanical stops the bit rising. Under upward pull it slides until the corners of the mouth stretch and, with enough force or a bit that is too wide, until the cheek is pressed against the first lower cheek tooth (Pollaris et al. 2025, cadavers). The lip is the stop.
  • The restraint is a hard stop for rotation: past about ° the shank stops turning. It is not a stop for force. Beyond that angle every extra newton on the rein becomes newtons on tongue and bars and a proportional squeeze of the chin.
  • The restraint is a rigid ring, not a chain: it engages almost at once, on a strip of skin a few millimetres wide, and has no give at all.
  • No chain: the only thing that halts the rotation is the cheekpiece going taut, and the mouthpiece travels up the face to get there. There is no mechanical limit before the corners of the mouth; a curb strap would add one.
  • A sliding gag has no stop of any kind unless stops are fitted to the cheekpieces. The bit rises as long as the rider pulls, up to the cheek teeth, with the corners of the mouth taking the whole lift.
  • The stop is the horse's own jaw bone: the ring or cage cannot pass it, so every increase in force is taken by the periosteum of the mandible and by whatever the mouthpiece lies on.
  • There is no stop and no release: the check is adjusted once, and the horse carries it for the whole session.
  • Rubber stops on the cheekpieces limit the slide to about .
  • With a strong one-sided pull the ring can be drawn into the mouth (pull-through guard ). Measured: in a cadaver study, one-sided rein tension displaced snaffles sideways, more so when the bit was too wide (Pollaris et al. 2025).
  • The ring passes through the mouthpiece. Not studied: whether the fold of the lip at the corner is caught in the gap and pinched as the ring turns. Rubber bit guards close the gap at the cost of thickness.
  • The cheek is squeezed from outside against its own teeth whenever a rein is used ( on the pad). Where the pad carries bristles or prongs, the contact is a few square millimetres and the skin is the limit.
  • The bit is strapped high in the mouth before any rein is taken, so the corners of the mouth carry a standing load and the mouthpiece cannot be dropped or released by the horse.

Head carriage and where the load goes

The same rein tension, held by a hand that does not yield, at six head carriages: not collected, above the bit, poll highest, on the vertical, behind the vertical, and hyperflexion. The column is the modelled peak pressure at each. Where one carriage is clearly lowest, that is the device’s mechanical preference in this model; where the columns are close, there is none. The model is static and holds tension equal across carriages, while in riding the tension changes with the carriage, so the lowest modelled peak is a property of the model, not a recommendation. The geometric escape, moving the mouth towards the hand, slackens any rein and is common to every bit: only the rider’s timing of release decides whether it is rewarded.

  • Lowest modelled peak with the neck long and the nose forward. The device is quietest when nothing is asked of the horse; it carries no instruction about a frame, and whatever carriage follows comes from the rider’s hand, not from the bit.
  • Lowest modelled peak with the head high and the nose out, at constant rein tension: as the head rises the load leaves the bars and tongue and moves to the corners of the mouth. Not studied: whether this draws a horse above the bit under a holding hand. It does not teach a correct carriage.
  • Lowest modelled peak with the poll up and the nose a little in front of the vertical. This device does not work against the carriage riders call correct; it still cannot teach it: only timely release does that.
  • Lowest modelled peak with the head on the vertical. The device is close to neutral; whether the horse ends up correct or curled depends on when the hand yields.
  • Lowest modelled peak behind the vertical, at constant rein tension. Not studied: whether this shapes carriage under a holding hand, or takes a horse that already goes behind the vertical further behind it.
  • Lowest modelled peak in hyperflexion, but only under this model’s assumption of equal rein tension. Measured: riding behind the vertical has been recorded with rein tension as high as or higher than in the competition frame (Christensen et al. 2014; König von Borstel et al. 2024), and hyperflexion narrows the airway, restricts forward vision and stretches the nuchal ligament (König von Borstel et al. 2024). Nothing in the metal prevents it; only the hand can.
  • No carriage preference is named. The table shows the modelled peak at each carriage for the same rein tension and a level rein. Measured: rein tension is as high or higher behind the vertical (Christensen et al. 2014; König von Borstel et al. 2024), and the rein is not level when the mouth is far below the hand; neither is modelled per carriage, so the lowest figure in the table is not a recommendation.
  • Curb action adds two downward pulls, chin back and poll down. From statics: in this model both are relieved by flexing at the poll, so a curb held without release favours the vertical; how far beyond it the horse goes depends on the hand.
  • Gag action lifts the corners of the mouth towards the poll. From statics: lifting is relieved by raising the head, so the bias is above the vertical, while the small poll component points the other way; the two signals conflict.
  • A check rein fixes the lowest position the head may take and resists every attempt below it; the carriage is not learned but enforced, for as long as the harness is on.
  • For a horse that already goes behind the vertical: this combination . The reliable remedy is the hand, not the metal: release when the nose comes forward, never when it comes back.

How the harm index and the class are built

Three parts, each 0–100. Pressure reading: the ratio of each modelled pressure to the derived pain threshold of the same tissue, on the worst tissue and on average over every loaded tissue. Injury expected: the likely lesions of Figure F, each weighted by how serious it is. Loss of control: no mechanical stop, no release, an ambiguous signal. The weights are judgements and are published in the catalogue. The class is read separately: it is the rein tension per side at which the most loaded tissue first reaches its derived pain threshold, set against rein tensions measured in horses (classes 0–3), or class 4 for a design built around a point load. Lesion risks are listed beside the class and do not set it.

    The model

    What is assumed, and where it comes from

    Everything above follows from static equilibrium, a contact model of mucosa on bone and pain thresholds derived from human measurements. The derivation and every parameter are published so that any number can be checked or changed.

    Forces

    Each shank is treated as a rigid lever pivoting at the mouthpiece. F is the whole tension the hand applies on one side; with two reins (a Pelham or a double bridle) it is split between the snaffle rein or bradoon and the curb rein in a measured proportion (MacKechnie-Guire et al. 2025), which the slider above can change. The rein pulls at the lower arm of length s at an angle θ to the head; once the shank has rotated by ρ, the chain (tension C) and the cheekpiece (tension T) hold it:

    F · s · sin(θ + ρ) = C · c + T · t

    where c and t are the perpendicular arms of the chain and the cheekpiece about the mouthpiece; the chain runs from the eye of the shank to the chin groove. The restoring moment is shared by chain and cheekpiece in proportion to stiffness times arm squared. Model assumption: the cheekpiece stiffness is chosen so that it takes a small share, in line with the small poll loads measured by Cross et al. (2017). For a plain Kimblewick the rein ring slides on the D and settles where the rein line passes through the centre of its arc. Without a chain the cheekpiece carries the whole moment, and the shank rotates until the lips stop the mouthpiece rising. For a pulley gag the strand to the poll carries F·e−μ(π−θ), friction over a wrap of π − θ. A ring around the jaw forms one body with the mouthpiece: the rein acts on the mouthpiece in full and the ring adds a clamp on the mandible whose size depends on fit (Model assumption). A combination bit routes a fixed share of the rein to nose, chin or jaw plates; a check rein is modelled as a lift along the face with the horse’s own push as the force; the bit’s weight is projected on the head axis at the chosen carriage. From statics: straps only pull. The component of the resultant towards the mandible lands on tongue and bars. The component towards the poll drives the mouthpiece into the corners of the mouth whatever the cheek, since fixed, full, D and hanging cheeks do not stop it (Manfredi et al. 2005); only a component towards the front of the mouth is held by the cheekpieces and passes to the poll.

    Pressures

    Over the bars the mouthpiece rests on 1–2 mm of mucosa bonded to bone, modelled as an elastic foundation of stiffness k = α·E/h, with α fitted to plane-strain finite-element runs. For a load q per unit length on a mouthpiece of radius R the contact half-width is a = (3qR/2k)1/3 and the mean pressure q/2a, so the strip widens as the load rises. Model assumption: the mucosa modulus is taken from human studies (Sawada 2011; Chen 2015); across their range the ratio of pressure to threshold hardly changes, because a softer layer both spreads the load and lowers the threshold. The tongue is very soft (shear modulus about 3 kPa in vivo, MR elastography in humans; Cheng 2011): once the mouthpiece has pressed the relaxed tongue down a few millimetres (Model assumption), most further load goes to the bars. Ports and arches leave the tongue room and send more load to the bars. A sharp or textured surface is modelled as a smaller surface radius, and a textured one touches over only part of its length; a chin chain and the headpiece press as a belt, p = C/(R·w). Not studied: no pressure-pain threshold has been measured in any horse’s mouth. Each tissue’s threshold is derived from human attached gingiva, 152 ± 62 kPa with a 4.8 mm probe (Baad-Hansen et al. 2013), for bars, lips and tongue (for the tongue no threshold has been measured in any species: Model assumption), with buccal mucosa 1.2 times and the palate 4.3 times higher (Ogawa et al. 2003; Ogimoto et al. 2002); and from human facial skin, 232 kPa with a 1 cm² probe (Magerl et al. 2010), for chin, nose, jaw and poll. The threshold is rescaled to the size of the contact, P* = Pref·(d/dref)n−2 with n = 0.7 from horse data (Taylor 2016; Pongratz & Licka 2017). Horse and human skin have the same epidermal thickness and nerve-fibre density (Tong et al. 2020); rescaled to 2–3 mm probes, the human facial threshold predicts 1.3–2.6 MPa, and living horses’ faces measured 1.0–3.8 MPa (Käfer-Karrer et al. 2026; Khatib et al. 2026). The readings are: below 0.5 times the threshold, well below; 0.5 to 1, approaching; 1 to 2, above; 2 or more, more than twice.

    Limits

    Static, two-dimensional, one horse of mean dimensions. Dynamic peaks, asymmetric reins, tongue movement, saliva and the horse’s own muscle tension are not modelled. The index weights are judgements and are shown as such. The model compares mechanisms with each other; it does not predict an individual horse’s pain or injury. Noseband tightness was a risk factor for lesions at the corners of the mouth and bit type was not (Uldahl & Clayton 2019); the noseband is not modelled. Pressures above 1500 kPa are shown as “> 1500 kPa”: beyond that, strains exceed what the linear contact model covers. The edge factors of textured mouthpieces are estimates, not measurements. The pain thresholds are derived from human data and checked against horses only for facial skin.
