Where it lands in the mouth

A section across the mouth at the interdental space, drawn to scale from the Helsinki measurements (mean mouth width 131 mm, 28.9 mm from palate to bars, tongue 16 mm thick, 1–2 mm of mucosa over the bone of the bars). The mouthpiece is drawn at its set thickness. Shading shows the modelled pressure on each tissue; numbers are kPa.

  • Thickness check: of metal in a space measured at . The tongue is compressed before any rein is taken up; the model adds to the tongue load for it.
  • Thickness check: in a space. The mouthpiece fits without crowding the tongue at rest.
  • Single joint: under tension the two cannons fold into a V. The joint sinks into the tongue (radiographs at 25 N show the tongue indenting rather than the palate being struck), the tongue is squeezed between the cannons, and the cannon ends bear on the bars at an angle. Palate contact becomes likely if the hand is high or the bit is too wide (risk index ).
  • Two joints: the centre piece lies on the tongue and the cannons follow its sides; the whole mouthpiece moves down onto the tongue instead of up towards the palate. Pressure is spread, not removed.
  • The flat plate stays kind only while it lies flat. Fixed cheeks held by keepers, or a rotated shank, tilt the plate so that its edge digs into the tongue: this is how a French link turns into a Dr. Bristol in the mouth.
  • Unjointed: the bar cannot fold, so the load is divided between the tongue (which it compresses) and the two bars (where it bridges onto thin mucosa over bone). Horses with unjointed bits show more bar lesions than horses with jointed bits in the Finnish trotter data.
  • The port relieves the tongue and sends that load to the bars. When the cheek rotates (curb action) the port swings upward; a medium or high port reaches the palate at ° of rotation (risk index ).
  • This mouthpiece is built to touch the palate: the spoon or U-piece stands tall and meets the roof of the mouth as soon as the cheek rotates or the head is pushed against it. Palate contact is the mechanism, not a side effect.
  • Textured surface: only of the surface touches tissue and the ridges concentrate the load (edge factor ). Thin mouthpieces also indent deeper before the bone stops them, so the contact strip on the bars is narrower.
  • Many links: the mouthpiece drapes over the tongue and cannot be leaned on, but each link is a small contact and the gaps between links can trap tongue or lip tissue.
  • Two strands with offset joints: the tongue is caught between four half-cannons that fold independently, and the load is carried on two thin lines instead of one.
  • The two halves cross and slide past each other: a one-sided rein draws one cannon across the tongue against the other. Pinching the tongue is the design.
  • A plate under the joint spreads the centre load over the tongue and stops the joint pinching it; the extended version also holds the tongue down, which is a restraint of the airway's main mover, not a comfort.
  • Keys hang from the bar and invite chewing. The bar itself is a straight bar; the keys add movement, not pressure relief.
  • A soft or covered mouthpiece conforms to the tongue and spreads the load; it is also thick, wears, and is chewed through, so it must be inspected for an exposed core.
  • The mouthpiece turns freely on its ring: pulled from below it presents its edge to the tongue and bars (edge factor raised to ), which is why a lead attached only to the ring is warned against.

Is there a stop, or does it keep going?

Two different questions. Does anything mechanical limit how far the bit moves in the mouth as the rider pulls harder, and does anything limit the force? A chain stops a shank rotating but not the force it transmits; a fixed cheek stops the mouthpiece riding up; a loose ring or a sliding gag has no stop at all before the corners of the mouth and the first cheek teeth; a ring around the jaw stops on bone.

  • The mouthpiece hangs from a fixed cheek, so it cannot ride up into the corners of the mouth: the bridle takes that component. Force itself is not limited; the rider can still apply any pressure to tongue and bars.
  • Nothing mechanical stops the bit rising. Under upward pull it slides until the corners of the mouth stretch and, with enough force or a wide bit, until the cheek is pressed against the first lower cheek tooth. The lip is the stop.
  • The restraint is a hard stop for rotation: past about ° the shank stops turning. It is not a stop for force. Beyond that angle every extra newton on the rein becomes newtons on tongue and bars and a proportional squeeze of the chin.
  • The restraint is a rigid ring, not a chain: it engages almost at once, on a strip of skin a few millimetres wide, and has no give at all.
  • No chain: the only thing that halts the rotation is the cheekpiece going taut, and the mouthpiece travels up the face to get there. There is no mechanical limit before the corners of the mouth; a curb strap would add one.
  • A sliding gag has no stop of any kind unless stops are fitted to the cheekpieces. The bit rises as long as the rider pulls, up to the cheek teeth, with the corners of the mouth taking the whole lift.
  • The stop is the horse's own jaw bone: the ring or cage cannot pass it, so every increase in force is taken by the periosteum of the mandible and by whatever the mouthpiece lies on.
  • There is no stop and no release: the check is adjusted once, and the horse carries it for the whole session.
  • Rubber stops on the cheekpieces limit the slide to about .
  • With a strong one-sided pull the ring can be drawn into the mouth (pull-through guard ). The cadaver study found unilateral tension displaces a loose ring snaffle the most.
  • The ring passes through the mouthpiece: the fold of the lip at the corner can be caught in the gap and pinched as the ring turns. Rubber bit guards remove this at the cost of thickness.
  • The cheek is squeezed from outside against its own teeth whenever a rein is used ( on the pad). Where the pad carries bristles or prongs, the contact is a few square millimetres and the skin is the limit.
  • The bit is strapped high in the mouth before any rein is taken, so the corners of the mouth carry a standing load and the mouthpiece cannot be dropped or released by the horse.

What the horse learns about its head

The same rein tension, held by a hand that does not yield, at six head carriages: not collected, above the bit, poll highest, on the vertical, behind the vertical, and hyperflexion. The column is the peak pressure the horse feels at each. A horse moves towards the carriage where it hurts least; that column is the device's own bias, before any training. The geometric escape, moving the mouth towards the hand (curling behind the vertical and on into hyperflexion), slackens any rein and is common to every bit: only the rider's timing of release decides whether it is rewarded.

  • Least pain with the neck long and the nose forward. The device is quietest when nothing is asked of the horse; it carries no instruction about a frame, and whatever carriage follows comes from the rider's hand, not from the bit.
  • Least pain with the head high and the nose out. Under a holding hand this combination teaches the horse to come above the bit: as the head rises the load leaves the bars and tongue and moves to the corners of the mouth, which hurt less. It does not teach a correct carriage; it teaches escape upward, with a hollow back behind it.
  • Least pain with the poll up and the nose a little in front of the vertical. Of the four carriages, this device hurts least at the one riders call correct, so it does not actively fight a correct frame. It still cannot teach it: only release does that.
  • Least pain with the head on the vertical. The device is close to neutral; whether the horse ends up correct or curled depends entirely on when the hand yields.
  • Least pain behind the vertical. The device itself rewards curling: as the horse tucks its nose the loads it carries fall away. A horse that already goes behind the vertical will be pushed further behind it by this combination under a holding hand.
  • Least pain in hyperflexion. The loads keep falling as the nose approaches the chest, so under a holding hand the device pays the horse for rollkur: the carriage that narrows the airway, blinds the horse to what is in front of it and loads the neck ligaments. Nothing in the metal stops it; only the hand can.
  • Curb action adds two downward instructions, chin back and poll down. Both are relieved by flexing the poll and lowering the head: the mechanical bias of every curb is towards the vertical and then behind it.
  • Gag action lifts the corners of the mouth towards the poll. Lifting is relieved by raising the head and opening the throat, so the bias is above the vertical; the small poll component says the opposite, which is why gags are described as giving the horse two instructions at once.
  • A check rein fixes the lowest position the head may take and punishes every attempt below it; the carriage is not learned but enforced, for as long as the harness is on.
  • For a horse that already goes behind the vertical: this combination . The reliable remedy is the hand, not the metal: release when the nose comes forward, never when it comes back.

How the harm index and the class are built

Three parts, each 0–100. Pain now: how far the modelled pressures stand above the pain thresholds, on the worst tissue and on average over every loaded tissue. Injury expected: the likely lesions of Figure F, each weighted by how serious it is (a torn commissure or bone damage of the bars counts for more than a chin gall). Loss of control: no mechanical stop, no release, an ambiguous signal. The weights are published in the catalogue. The class is not an average: it is the worst of the pain reading, the worst lesion risk and the design, so one expected injury puts a combination in the red whatever the rest says.

    • Within this register it sits in the middle: not built to hurt, but with at least one mechanism (listed above) that concentrates load or removes a stop.
    • Within this register it is among the harshest arrangements. Its design relies on pain or the credible threat of it, and at measured riding tensions it exceeds the human facial pain threshold on at least one tissue.
    • Every bit, this one included, works by negative reinforcement: an aversive pressure is applied and removed when the horse complies. The register measures how aversive, how precisely it can be removed, and whether anything protects the horse when the hand does not remove it.
    • Without a mechanical stop against rising, the only protection for the corners of the mouth is the rider's restraint. In a moment of fright or resistance there is none.

    The model

    What is assumed, and where it comes from

    Everything above follows from static equilibrium and a simple contact model. The derivation and every parameter are published so that any number can be checked or changed.

    Forces

    Each shank is treated as a rigid lever pivoting at the mouthpiece. The rein pulls at the lower arm of length s with tension F at an angle θ to the head; the chain (if present) pulls at the upper arm of length p. Moment balance after the shank has rotated by ρ:

    F · s · sin(θ + ρ) = C · p · cos ρ + T · p · sin ρ

    With a chain the cheekpiece carries 15 % of the moment and the chain the rest, as the direct force measurements of Cross and colleagues indicate. Without a chain the cheekpiece carries all of it, and the shank rotates until the lips stop the mouthpiece rising. For a sliding gag the cheekpiece is a cord over a pulley: the strand to the poll carries F·e−μπ/2. A ring around the jaw turns the pull into a clamp on the mandible; a combination bit routes a fixed share of the rein to nose, chin or jaw plates before the mouthpiece; a check rein is modelled as a lift along the face with the horse's own push as the force. The tissues receive the vector sum: the component towards the mandible lands on tongue and bars, the component towards the poll on the corners of the mouth (less what a fixed cheek takes).

    Pressures

    A mouthpiece of radius r pressed into tissue to a depth δ touches a strip of width 2√(2rδ − δ²). Over the bars the depth is limited by the 1.5 mm of mucosa over bone; over the tongue 5 mm is assumed. Width times contact length times the fraction of surface that actually touches (less than one for twisted, serrated or linked surfaces) is the contact area; the load divided by it, times an edge factor for ridges, tilted plates or a free-pivoting bar, is the pressure. Thresholds come from the comparison of equestrian rein pressures with human pressure-pain thresholds.

    Limits

    Static, two-dimensional, one horse of mean dimensions. Dynamic peaks, asymmetric reins, tongue movement, saliva, and the horse's own tension are not modelled. The index weights are judgements and are shown as such. The model ranks mechanisms against each other; it does not predict an individual horse's injury.
