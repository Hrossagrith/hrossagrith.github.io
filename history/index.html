Early history

From the Crossing to the Compact

Four reconstructed parts covering c. 1200–100 BC. The account does not begin with a desired modern territory, and it does not grant the Empire a national birth the evidence cannot carry.

Historical method. This reconstruction combines real archaeological constraints with a fictional Hrossagrith historical tradition. Archaeology can constrain environments, technologies, settlement patterns and material exchange; it cannot verify the particular feuds, decisions, speeches or motives recorded here. Where the surviving tradition cannot distinguish among several explanations, the account preserves that uncertainty rather than presenting one motive as established fact.

Part 1 · c. 1200–650 BC

  1. c. 1200–1050 BC

    Before the Hrossagrith people

    There is no Hrossagrith people, state or common identity. The central Irish lowlands hold scattered farming communities around the middle Shannon, Lough Ree, central Westmeath, eastern Roscommon and northern Offaly. Authority belongs to households, kin groups and local leaders rather than permanent states. Horses may be present in small numbers, but they are not yet the basis of society, law or government — and the region that later becomes the Hrossagrith core is one ordinary inland area among several wealthier ones.

    Territorial status: no Compact, no shared territory.

  2. c. 1050–980 BC

    The Crossing Crisis

    The first lasting divergence is placed at an important crossing on the middle Shannon — already more than a practical route, and used for seasonal exchange, marriage arrangements, ritual gatherings, dispute settlement and the movement of livestock and travellers.

    A succession of disputes over stolen cattle, access to the crossing and old killings widens into a feud. Retaliation spreads beyond the original families; travellers are attacked, repairs stop, and the crossing becomes unusable. Everyone nearby suffers: livestock cannot be moved safely, traders avoid the route, and isolated settlements become vulnerable.

    Across several later gatherings, influential households repeatedly restore and extend a temporary peace custom — approximately, a prohibition on killing or seizing an accepted participant during a gathering or on its recognised approaches. The protection is narrow: only during the gathering, only in recognised places, only for people the hosts accept. Outside that time and place, the feud may continue.

Part 2 · c. 650–500 BC

Practices later grouped under the label Sanctuary Compact continue, but the surviving tradition does not prove uninterrupted institutional continuity or an orderly state. What can be reconstructed is a small and unreliable network repeatedly recreated through dealings among settlements, households, travellers and people able to provide protection. Participating communities share no national name, monarch, capital, written law or fixed border.

  1. c. 650–620 BC

    Survival and unreliable memory

    One group repairs a crossing, another keeps boats ready, another clears higher paths over gravel ridges, another stores fodder. The result is an uneven network of usable routes, not a planned road system. There is no written archive: important agreements are repeated publicly before witnesses and marked by exchanged objects, pledged livestock or recognised landmarks. Witnesses die, objects are lost, powerful households intimidate people, and settlements preserve incompatible versions of the same promise.

    Sanctuary also fails. Protected travellers are occasionally robbed or killed, sometimes by people connected to their own hosts. What makes sanctuary valuable is not that it is never broken, but that breaking it can bring retaliation from several unrelated communities and damage future trade.

  2. c. 620–590 BC

    Craft households and disputed dependence

    Iron objects and knowledge arrive unevenly, through several people and routes rather than one technological change; bronze stays valuable and there is no dependable Compact iron industry. Travelling craft households are welcomed selectively for their skills, and sponsors provide food, protection and a place to work in exchange for labour, goods, teaching or a share of production — arrangements that repeatedly cause disputes when the amount, duration or consent was never clearly established.

    Over decades a custom strengthens: a person accepted under protection should not be separated from accompanying household members, or deprived of protected animals and essential objects, before the dispute is brought before witnesses. It also protects sponsors, by requiring people leaving an arrangement to account for entrusted materials. Powerful sponsors sometimes ignore it entirely.

  3. c. 590–550 BC

    Escort payment disputes and combined backing

    Households providing escorts, boats and lookouts demand more compensation. They are not professional soldiers but farming and herding households diverting labour and food while risking lives. When an influential escort lineage is accused of taking additional payments from travellers already promised protection, hosts object that this turns a guarantee into extortion. Neither side secures a lasting victory.

    What emerges instead is a durable practical lesson: agreements backed by both kinds of social power endure — people able to assemble witnesses and negotiate obligations, and people able to organise escorts, retaliation and defence. This is a habit, not a diarchy, and it produces no office.

  4. c. 550–500 BC

    A durable but disputed network

    Households from different origins are connected by marriages, fostered children, debts, feuds and craft ties, but no clear common identity survives in the record. Some expectations outlast the emergencies that created them: aid is repaid, jointly maintained crossings are not obstructed without warning, and attacking a protected guest may draw retaliation from communities unrelated to the victim.

    Influential customs continue to reject permanent human ownership and the transfer of people as property. Detention and temporary hostages occur, but those held remain protected persons who must be released when the obligation is fulfilled or formally abandoned.

    Horses remain poorly evidenced and socially limited, and are not yet central to any identity. Cattle matter more economically; boats and travel on foot are more visible as transport. Safe-conduct customs protect animals travelling with a household, including any horses present, because their seizure can trap or impoverish protected people.

    Territorial status: a fragile protection network around the middle Shannon, without the name Hrossagrið, a monarch, either Crown, written law, a permanent army, a capital, a continuous border or an Atlantic harbour.

A promise of protection means little unless someone can make it physically real, while force without a recognised promise becomes extortion.

Part 3 · c. 500–300 BC

  1. c. 500–460 BC

    Renewal without centralisation

    Sanctuary practices continue because they solve recurring problems: strangers need sponsors, traders need safe stopping places, hosts need compensation, and neighbours need ways to end feuds without surrendering entirely. One seasonal gathering becomes more regular — not because anyone founds a capital, but because its location has water, grazing and familiar approaches, and host households compete for the prestige it brings. In years of shortage it shrinks, moves, or is abandoned.

    A stronger expectation develops that major agreements should name the people involved, describe the obligation, identify witnesses and state what ends the arrangement. Terms are still remembered orally and remain easy to distort; a version heard often is not necessarily the original.

  2. c. 460–420 BC

    The Three-Routes Agreement

    The original crossing becomes a bottleneck in good seasons and a point of failure in bad ones. Influential households try to connect three alternatives: the old crossing, an upstream boat passage, and an east–west path over firmer raised ground. Trusted speakers meet across several seasons to coordinate escort contributions, boat repair, compensation for sanctuary breaches, emergency grazing and recognisable warnings.

    It works for a time, because no single route is indispensable. Its success then creates new conflicts: contributions are unequal, households near the busiest crossing collect more gifts and prestige, and contributors answer free-riding by reserving boats and escorts for people named in their own agreements. The agreement does not collapse in a moment — contributions become irregular, gatherings are attended selectively, and within a generation common coordination has mostly failed.

    Territorial status: three routes briefly connected by overlapping promises and shared labour. They never become one governed district.

  3. c. 420–370 BC

    Fodder years and the limits of refuge

    Several damaging local seasons within a generation — flooded grazing, fodder shortage, livestock disease, interrupted exchange — are adopted as a plausible compound shock rather than a dated climate event. The effects are uneven, and the refuge network cannot accept everyone. Hosts ration protection: skilled workers and people with marriage connections are more likely to be admitted than unsupported families.

    The pressure exposes the limits of the anti-ownership custom. Sponsors try to convert unpaid food debts into open-ended service, or to keep younger household members after an agreement ends. The conflict strengthens an uneven rule that continued service requires renewal before witnesses, with the person expected to serve present and their assent heard — though pressure can still make assent a fiction.

  4. c. 370–330 BC

    Iron, migrants and competing routes

    Iron tools become more familiar but not suddenly common; bronze retains practical and symbolic value. Movement across the Irish Sea brings people, techniques and objects, intermittently and in limited numbers — no invasion, no population replacement, and no proof of a fourth-century language change. Wealth differences grow: certain host families accumulate cattle, metal objects, foreign ornaments and marriage connections, and present generosity as proof that they deserve to mediate disputes.

    There is still no coinage, no network-wide standard of measurement, and no authority able to dictate exchange terms. Attempts by one household to make its own containers or valuations compulsory provoke suspicion, because whoever defines the measure can favour themselves.

  5. c. 330–300 BC

    Prestige goods and politically significant horse keeping

    A few wealthy protection households gradually divert grazing, winter fodder and labour to horses, because repeated route disputes make speed valuable. Breeding does not quickly enlarge the stock: a mare carries a foal through most of a year, and a surviving young horse consumes several seasons before it can carry an urgent rider. A concentration can vanish within a generation through injury, disease or the loss of a mare.

    The advantage is nevertheless political. A mounted messenger can warn a crossing, summon relatives, or reach a gathering before a rival account of a dispute. But speed does not make a message true: later witnesses may contradict it, and hosts may treat an unsupported mounted report as manipulation.

    Territorial status by c. 300 BC: a recurring protection network with more recognised customs, a few wealthy lineages, uneven ironworking — and still no common national name, monarch, Crown, permanent assembly, written law, army, taxation, coinage, city or fixed border.

Part 4 · c. 300–100 BC

  1. c. 300–260 BC

    The cost of acting first

    Mounted messengers create an immediate political problem, and the answer is not a mounted ruling class. Suitable horses are scarce, and owning one is not the same as keeping it usable: fodder must be stored, injuries managed, riders must learn the ground. Wet ground, flooding, darkness and water crossings erase much of the speed advantage.

    The deeper change is that important hosts increasingly refuse to treat first arrival as proof. A messenger may secure temporary precautions, but a lasting judgement waits for named witnesses or a second account — which reduces the benefit of racing to establish a false story first.

  2. c. 260–220 BC

    The horse-guarantee disputes

    Horse seizure becomes politically charged in a way the loss of an ordinary animal is not. Cattle remain far more important to most households as wealth and food, but taking a rival’s horse removes speed, prestige, and the ability to summon allies. There is still no general doctrine that horses are legally unlike cattle, and communities outside the network may see no reason to recognise any special distinction at all.

    Territorial status: protected places and routes embedded among independently controlled lands. A person may move from one recognised host to another without ever entering a continuous jurisdiction.

On the name, and on the Crowns. Throughout this period the name Hrossagrið does not exist; it emerges much later as an Old Norse outsider-name. Likewise, the recurring distinction between people who negotiate obligations and people who organise protection is treated by historians only as a faint cultural ancestor of the Guiding and Guarding Crowns, which emerge much later from separate crises. No unbroken office connects them.
